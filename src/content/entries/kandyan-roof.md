## What it meant

The Kandyan roof is an answer to weather. Steep hipped roofs, often in two tiers, are covered with small flat clay tiles (pethi ulu) and carried on timber pillars and brackets set on stone plinths. Rain runs off fast, and the timber stays clear of wet ground.

The same grammar shapes humbler buildings. A tampita viharaya is a small timber shrine raised on stone pillars, protecting it from damp and termites. An ambalama is an open-sided wayside pavilion where travellers could rest.

## How it changed

It never settled into one style. Gadaladeniya, in the 14th century, blends Sinhala and Dravidian forms. At Embekke Devale the craftsmanship moves into wood, with famed carving on its pillars and brackets.

The Temple of the Tooth shows how a royal building accumulates. Kirti Sri Rajasinha (1747–82) expanded it; Sri Vikrama Rajasinha (1798–1815), the last Kandyan king, added the octagonal Pattirippuwa, originally a royal audience pavilion. The roofline you photograph is the work of several reigns.

## Notice this

At Embekke, look up. Much of the carving is on the brackets overhead, where a camera pointed at eye level misses it entirely.

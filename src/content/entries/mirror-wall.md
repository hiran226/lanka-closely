## What it meant

Sigiriya is a granite plug rising about 180 to 200 m from the plain, made a royal seat by Kashyapa I, who reigned 477–495 CE. The chronicle says he fled to Sihagiri, walled it and built a lion staircase. The two colossal paws at the final ascent are what remains of that lion: you once climbed in through its mouth.

Partway up, the Mirror Wall runs along the rock face: brick masonry finished with highly polished white plaster. Above it, in a sheltered pocket on the western face, are the painted women.

## How it changed

From about the 8th to 10th centuries, visitors climbed up, looked, and scratched verses into the plaster. Senerat Paranavitana deciphered and published 685 of them in *Sigiri Graffiti* (1956); estimates of the total run to 1,500 or more. They count among the oldest Sinhala poetry. The paintings fared worse: only a fraction of what was once there survives.

## Notice this

The vandals became the archive. Without their scratches we would know far less about how early visitors felt standing exactly where you are.

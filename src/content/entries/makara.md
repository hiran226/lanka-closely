## What it meant

The balustrade either side of a sacred stair is the korawak gala, or wingstone. Its signature ornament is the makara: an open mouth at the top, a body that scrolls down to make the rail.

The makara is assembled from parts of other animals, and one commonly repeated reading, given for Lankatilaka, assigns each a virtue: an elephant's trunk for dexterity, a lion's paws for strength, a crocodile's jaws for discipline, a boar's ears for sensitivity, a fish's body for movement and a peacock's feathers for beauty. Treat the list as a mnemonic rather than a rulebook, but it is a good one.

## How it changed

The creature did not stay on the stairs. In the makara torana, two makara heads sit at the ends of an arch, their bodies forming the curve, often with a kirtimukha face at the apex. At Lankatilaka in Polonnaruwa a Buddha image stands within one. At Degaldoruwa, in the Kandyan period, the same arch appears in paint as well as carving, a stone idea translated into colour.

## Notice this

Stand at the foot of the stair and look up. The rail you would steady yourself on is the makara's body, and the gaping mouth is waiting at the top.

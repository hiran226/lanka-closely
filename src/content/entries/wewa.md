## What it meant

A wewa is an earthen reservoir: a long bund thrown across a shallow valley, a sluice to let water out and a spillway for floods. Few stand alone. In the cascaded tank-village system (ellangawa), tanks in one micro-catchment are linked so that the overflow of the upper tank feeds the tanks and fields below. The system evolved over nearly two millennia, and the FAO recognised it in 2017.

A line traditionally attributed to Parakramabahu I, a paraphrase of the chronicle's sentiment rather than his recorded words, sums up the ethic: let not a drop of rain reach the sea without first serving people.

## How it changed

The engineering grew precise. The bisokotuwa, a stone-lined valve pit, regulated outflow and eased water pressure at the sluice; it was in use in the ancient period, though an exact date is best not pinned down.

The great tanks followed: Minneriya under Mahasena in the 3rd century, Kala Wewa under Dhatusena in the 5th, Parakrama Samudra in the 12th. Kala Wewa's bisokotuwa feeds the Yodha Ela, a canal of about 87 km to Tissa Wewa at Anuradhapura.

## Notice this

Over its first 17 miles the Yodha Ela falls about six inches per mile, roughly 10 cm per kilometre: a slope you could not see, but water can.
